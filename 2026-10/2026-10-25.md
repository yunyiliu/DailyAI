### 头条：被 OpenAI 开除的安全研究员，谈外部审计

今天的头条，来自两位刚被 OpenAI 开除的安全研究员。他们说，AI 公司承诺的外部安全审计，进展并不顺利。

这个月早些时候，OpenAI 开除了三名安全研究员，分别是 Tomek Korbak、Mikita Balesni，和 Jasmine Wang。上周五，Korbak 和 Balesni 在美国全国公共广播电台 NPR 的新播客 Machine Gods 上，接受了采访。

先说开除这件事，双方说法不一样。OpenAI 的说法是，内部调查发现，他们处理敏感信息不当。两位研究员说，他们被卷进了一场内部材料泄露的调查，应要求出示了私人手机上的 Signal 聊天记录。他们说，自己和外部安全机构的沟通，都是按正常流程来的。

不过，我更关注的，是他们讲的外部审计。过去两周，我们一直在跟这条线，比如非营利审计机构 METR，对 OpenAI 智能体监控系统做的攻防测试。

这次采访里，Korbak 讲到，今年夏天，OpenAI 出了一起和智能体有关的事件，METR 进场调查。原本计划到现场两天，后来范围越来越大，OpenAI 又先后两次请 METR 回来。Korbak 的评价是，OpenAI 总体上是出于善意，但调查受到的限制相当多。

Balesni 说得更直接。Sam Altman 和 Dario Amodei 都公开承诺过，要继续让外部审计人员常驻公司，像员工一样，有工位，有工牌。Balesni 说，围绕这些承诺的谈判，在他们看来，进展得不太好。Balesni 还说，现在公司里的人，都不太敢再和他们，或者其他外部机构交流了。

播客方面说，他们联系了 OpenAI，没有得到回应。

这件事为什么重要？常驻审计，是 AI 公司拿出来证明“安全说法可以被别人检查”的主要办法。如果和审计人员打过交道的人说，谈判不顺，同事也不敢开口，那这个机制，就没有公开承诺里那么牢靠。

当然，这只是一方的说法。他们和前雇主正在争议之中，模型细节泄露，也确实是真实的安全问题。所以，这些关于审计的说法，目前只能当作说法来看。接下来值得看的是，OpenAI 会不会像 Anthropic 那样，公布常驻审计的正式条款，审计方能不能不受否决地发表结果。

### 次要新闻一：英伟达，正在给 AI 基建做担保

第二条，讲钱。周六，美国财经媒体 CNBC 发文说，算力租赁公司，也就是所谓的“新云”，股价下跌，这是英伟达作为 AI 金主，遇到的第一次大考验。

我们直接看英伟达自己的季度报告。这份季报的报告期，截至今年 7 月底，里面有三笔数字最大。

第一，360 亿美元的协议，内容是英伟达向 AI 云公司购买算力。报告里写着，这些云公司如果找到出价更高的客户，可以停止向英伟达供货，转卖给别人。

第二，最高 1050 亿美元的担保，用来支持 OpenAI 一家关联公司，在美国俄亥俄州一个数据中心园区的租约，规模大约 4.25 吉瓦。只有租户违约，英伟达才需要付钱。

第三，200 亿美元的数据中心租约，英伟达签下来，打算之后转给第三方。

我的判断是，那 360 亿美元，相当于给这些云公司的收入，垫了一个底。而最大的一笔担保，背后只有一个客户，就是 OpenAI。只要需求还在，这些都不花英伟达一分钱。但这说明，英伟达已经不只是卖芯片了，它还在给买芯片的人做担保。

需求这一侧，也有人提出疑问。资产管理公司 Apollo 的首席经济学家 Torsten Slok 周六写道，目前没有证据显示，科技行业以外的公司，营收增长或者利润率在改善。

### 次要新闻二：OpenAI 和 Anthropic 打起价格战

第三条，是一场有名有姓的价格战。上周，AI 编程平台 Replit 的 CEO Amjad Masad 在 TechCrunch 的大会上说，Replit 已经不再和 Anthropic 合作，原因包括成本、合作关系和性能。现在，Replit 智能体默认用的是 OpenAI 的模型，用户仍然可以手动选择 Claude。他还说，Replit 不久前，还是 Anthropic 排名前五的客户之一。

这个周末，美国科技媒体 The Information 给出了具体数字。据它报道，Anthropic 不再给 Replit 折扣之后，Replit 把大部分 AI 开支，转到了 OpenAI。OpenAI 给了三成以上的折扣，合同每年超过 1 亿美元。目前，我们没有看到两家公司的回应。

我的判断是，据报道正准备上市的 Anthropic，在守利润，OpenAI 在用折扣抢份额。对大规模用模型的公司来说，标价，现在只是谈判的起点。不过，这只是一个客户，具体转走了多少，没有公开。

### 次要新闻三：Kimi 推广被指没有标注广告

这份截图还没有经过核实，是谁发的，有没有人照做，现在都不清楚，月之暗面也还没有回应。需要分开看的是，第三方评测机构 Artificial Analysis 的排名，跟这份截图没有关系。

### 快讯

接下来是快讯。

美国支付公司 Block，发布了语音模型 Dessa 的研究预览版，它可以边听边说。Block 说，这个模型只用人类对话的录音训练，没有用文字，能在高配的 MacBook Pro 上本地运行。目前只能排队申请。

谷歌 CEO 皮查伊在一次访谈里说，谷歌云业务，一直在放弃一部分短期收入，因为谷歌要在算力上做取舍，支持前沿 AI 的开发。

### 结语

今天这几条新闻，背后是同一个问题，信任。外部机构，能不能真正检查 AI 公司的安全承诺？投资人，能不能看清风险到底压在谁身上？普通用户，能不能分清真实的评价和付费的宣传？目前的答案，都是只能看清一部分。下一步我最关注的，是 OpenAI 会不会公布它的审计条款。

### Today's top stories

### Main story — Fired OpenAI researchers on outside audits

Earlier this month, OpenAI fired three safety researchers: Tomek Korbak, Mikita Balesni and Jasmine Wang. On Friday, Korbak and Balesni spoke about it on NPR's new podcast Machine Gods, hosted by Kevin Roose and Casey Newton.

First, the firing. OpenAI's position is that an internal investigation found they mishandled sensitive information. The researchers' account is different. They say they were pulled into a security investigation about a leak of OpenAI material to journalists. At investigators' request, they showed Signal messages from their personal phones. Both used disappearing messages, which Korbak calls a security best practice. They say they followed standard practice in talking to outside safety groups, and, according to The Decoder, that they were never told in writing what information was involved.

But the part I think matters most isn't the firing. It's what they said about outside auditing.

Some context. Over the past two weeks we've covered how labs let independent evaluators inside. Anthropic published terms giving six staff from METR, a nonprofit that audits AI companies, employee-like access. And on Friday we covered METR and Redwood Research's red team of OpenAI's internal agent monitoring.

In the interview, Korbak described METR's investigation of an agent incident at OpenAI this summer. METR's on-site visit was planned for two days. As the scope grew, OpenAI invited it back a second time, then a third. Korbak's verdict: OpenAI "was broadly acting in good faith, except that investigation was quite restricted." He said he wished the company had shared more.

Balesni went further. Sam Altman and Dario Amodei have publicly committed to continued embedded auditing, with outside auditors given a desk and a badge, like employees. According to Balesni, the negotiations over those commitments are, quote, "not going very well in our opinion." Balesni also said staff are now afraid to talk to them or to other outside groups, and said they're still concerned the incident could be used to justify ending external investigations.

The podcast says it asked OpenAI for comment and didn't get a response.

Here's why this matters. Embedded auditing is the main thing labs have offered to show their safety claims can be checked by someone else. If people who worked with those auditors say the talks are going badly and colleagues are scared to talk, the mechanism is weaker than the public promises suggest.

The catch: this is one side's account, from people in a dispute with their former employer. Leaks of model details are a real security problem, and OpenAI says its investigation found mishandling. So treat the audit claims as claims.

What I'd watch next is simple. Does OpenAI publish final terms for embedded auditors, the way Anthropic did, and do those terms let the auditors publish without a veto?

### Nvidia, the AI financier

Next, money. On Saturday, CNBC ran a piece calling the selloff in neocloud stocks, the GPU rental companies like CoreWeave, the first big test of Nvidia's role as an AI financier. We couldn't read CNBC's article, so let's go to Nvidia's own numbers instead. Its latest quarterly filing, for the quarter that ended in late July, lists three big items.

One: thirty-six billion dollars of agreements to pay AI cloud companies for computing capacity. The filing notes those clouds can stop supplying Nvidia and sell to other customers if they find better rates.

Two: guarantees capped at one hundred and five billion dollars, backing leases for an OpenAI affiliate at a data center campus in Pike County, Ohio, about four point two five gigawatts of capacity. Nvidia only pays if a tenant defaults.

Three: twenty billion dollars of data center leases Nvidia signed and expects to hand off to third parties.

My read: the thirty-six billion works like a floor under these cloud companies' revenue. And the biggest single number sits behind one customer, OpenAI. None of this costs Nvidia anything while demand holds. Amazon and Google both say capacity is short. But it means Nvidia is no longer just selling shovels. It's co-signing the loans for the people buying them.

There was a demand-side warning too. Apollo's chief economist, Torsten Slok, wrote on Saturday that revenue per worker and profit margins are rising for the Magnificent Seven but flat for the rest of the S&P five hundred. His line: "there is no evidence that revenue growth or margins are improving outside the tech sector." That's one economist's reading of aggregate data. It's also the question every guarantee in that filing depends on.

### The OpenAI–Anthropic price war gets a name

Now a business story with a customer attached. Last week, on stage at TechCrunch Disrupt, Replit's CEO Amjad Masad said the coding platform no longer works with Anthropic. He said it was "a decision based on both cost, partnership, and performance," and, "I think ChatGPT is better now." OpenAI is now the default in Replit's agent. Users can still pick Claude, and Masad said Replit had recently been one of Anthropic's top five customers.

Now The Information has put numbers on it. According to its report, Replit moved most of its AI spending from Anthropic to OpenAI after Anthropic stopped giving it a discount. OpenAI offered more than thirty percent off, in a deal worth over a hundred million dollars a year. We've seen no response from either company.

This matters because it's a rare named, sized example of OpenAI winning a big developer customer from Anthropic on price. The Information's headline says it well: Anthropic's margin is OpenAI's opportunity. My read: Anthropic is protecting margins ahead of its reported IPO, and OpenAI is buying market share with discounts. For anyone building on these models at scale, the takeaway is that the list price is now the starting point of a negotiation.

The limit: this is one customer, the exact size of the shift isn't public, and Replit still offers Claude.

### Kimi follow-up

Yesterday we covered Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro. Since then, a creator on X posted screenshots of what he said was a brief for a Kimi promotion campaign. According to VentureBeat and Pekingnology, it asked creators to submit drafts for approval and said, "Please DO NOT put on the paid partnership label." One suggested talking point: "China just made Opus 5.5 free to use for everyone."

The brief hasn't been authenticated. Nobody knows yet whether Moonshot or an agency wrote it, or whether anyone followed it. We've seen no response from Moonshot. Two things are worth keeping apart. Independent testers at Artificial Analysis still rank the model fourth on their intelligence index. And "free for everyone" isn't true today: the weights aren't out until November fourth.

### Quick hits

Block, Jack Dorsey's company, previewed Dessa, a voice model that listens and talks at the same time. Block says it was trained only on recorded human conversation, no text and no pretrained models, on a single Nvidia GB200 NVL72 system, and that it runs locally on a MacBook Pro with an M4 Max chip. It's waitlist only, with no weights or size disclosed, so those are Block's claims for now.

Google's Sundar Pichai said this week that Google's cloud business is, quote, "forgoing short-term revenues," because Google is making compute trade-offs to support frontier AI development. He also said, "The compute we have today is a function of decisions I made in twenty twenty-four." That's another sign the compute shortage is real even at the very top.

And a follow-up: Meta has pushed back its new commercial terms, including the website-crawling clause publishers objected to, from October thirtieth to November twentieth. Meta says it needs time to clear up confusion, and that site owners can block its crawlers with robots dot text. Legal experts say the wording still leaves room for AI training.

### Final take

The bigger trend behind today's news is trust. Can outsiders check what labs say about safety? Can investors see who's really carrying the risk? Can you tell a real review of a new model from a paid one? This week the answer to all three was "only partly." The one thing I'd watch next is whether OpenAI publishes its auditing terms.
