### 开场

### 头条：三天，三个数学大结论，还没专家确认

先说头条。三天之内，三家 AI 实验室，各自公布了一个数学或计算机理论上的重大结论。到现在，我们还没看到有人类专家，公开确认其中任何一个是对的。

周一，Anthropic 在 GitHub 上公布了一篇论文和一套形式化证明，说证明了 VP 不等于 VNP。这是 Valiant 在 1979 年提出的问题，常被称为 P 和 NP 问题的代数版本。但它是另一个问题，解决了它，不等于解决了 P 和 NP。说明文件写着，结果是一个内部研究用的 Claude 模型发现的，还没有人类数学家检查过这个证明。

周二，OpenAI 公布了一篇预印本，题目是，矩阵乘法指数是四分之九。这个指数，衡量的是两个大矩阵相乘，计算量随规模增长得有多快。课本上的方法是三次方。今年 10 月，OpenAI 的另一篇预印本说，可以降到四分之九，也就是 2.25。这次的新论文说的是另一半，在一种叫形式算术电路的计算模型里，不可能再低了。OpenAI 说，论文出自一个没有公开名字的内部模型。

周三，谷歌研究院一天之内放出了 6 篇论文，都来自一个叫 Cogentic 的 Agent 系统。谷歌说，最受关注的那篇，底层模型是 Gemini 4，除了最开始的提示，没有任何人工参与。这篇论文，是关于大数分解的，也就是 RSA 加密背后的那个难题。现在实际最快的方法叫数域筛法，它的速度从来没有被严格证明过，依赖一些没证明的假设。谷歌这篇论文说，它的算法速度在同一个量级，而且不需要任何未经证明的假设。

三天，三个大结论。真正要问的是，到底检查到了哪一步？

Anthropic 的证明，全部写进了一个叫 Lean 的证明检查工具，每一步都由机器验证过。但说明文件自己也写了，机器检查的是证明，没有机器检查这个文件说的是不是声称的那件事。定义 VP 和 VNP 的那个文件，一共 98 行，还需要人类专家确认，这些定义就是标准的定义。

OpenAI 的机器证明，覆盖的范围比论文窄。它证明的是不带除法的电路，而论文的主定理允许除法。OpenAI 的仓库自己也提醒，部分没有形式化的结果，可能有问题。

谷歌说，分解算法的完整 Lean 证明已经完成，会在之后单独发布。现在文件夹里，只有论文。

这个顺序，是按外人今天能检查多少来排的，不代表哪个结论更可能是对的。三篇都没有经过同行评审。我们也还没找到有名有姓的外部专家，审过 OpenAI 的新下界，或者 Anthropic 的那个定义文件。

实际影响，比标题小得多。我的判断是，矩阵乘法这个结果，讲的是一种数学上的计算模型，不是现实中的计算速度，你的 GPU 不会因此变快。谷歌分解算法的速度公式里，有一个常数是 105。所以，这是理论成果，不会让任何人的 RSA 密钥变得不安全。

真正值得注意的，是数量。OpenAI 的数学仓库里，已经有 730 份手稿。谷歌一天发了 6 篇。产出结论变便宜了，检查结论成了瓶颈。

接下来看三件事，代数复杂性的专家，会不会确认那 98 行定义，谷歌会不会公开 Lean 证明，还有，有没有人在 OpenAI 的下界里找到漏洞。

### Hook

### Main story: three math claims, three levels of checking

Here's what happened, in order.

On Monday, Anthropic posted a paper and a formal proof in its public GitHub repository, claiming that VP is not equal to VNP. That's Leslie Valiant's question from 1979, often described as the algebraic cousin of P versus NP. It's a separate problem, so solving it doesn't settle P versus NP. The README says the result was discovered by "an internal research Claude model," and that "no human mathematician has checked the proof."

On Tuesday, OpenAI posted a preprint titled "The matrix multiplication exponent is nine fourths." This exponent measures how the cost of multiplying two big matrices grows as they get larger. The schoolbook method is cubic. Researchers have been pushing the exponent down since 1969. An earlier OpenAI preprint, in October, claimed you can get it down to two and a quarter. The new paper claims the other half: that you can't do better, at least in a specific mathematical model of computation called formal arithmetic circuits. OpenAI says the paper came from an unnamed internal model.

On Wednesday, Google Research posted six papers in a single day, all from an agent system called Cogentic. For the headline paper, Google says Cogentic ran on Gemini 4, with no human interaction other than the initial prompt. That paper claims a factoring algorithm with a proof behind its running time. That's the problem underneath RSA encryption. The fastest method people actually use, the number field sieve, has a similar running-time shape, but nobody has proved it runs that fast. It rests on unproven assumptions. Google's paper says it needs none.

So three big claims in three days. The question that matters is: how much has actually been checked?

Anthropic's proof is formalized in Lean, a proof checker, so a machine has verified every step. But the README itself names the gap: Lean checks the proofs, "but no machine checks that this file says what is claimed." That file, the one defining VP and VNP, is ninety-eight lines long. A human expert still has to confirm that those definitions are the standard ones.

OpenAI's Lean proof covers a narrower case than the paper. It proves the result for circuits without division, while the paper's main theorem allows division. OpenAI's own repository warns that "some of the unformalized results could have issues."

Google says a complete Lean formalization of the factoring proof exists and "will be released in a companion submission." Right now, the folder has the paper and nothing else.

That's an order of how much an outsider can check today. It's not an order of which result is most likely to be right.

None of the three is peer reviewed. And we haven't found a named outside expert who has reviewed the new OpenAI lower bound or Anthropic's statement file.

The practical side is simpler than the headlines suggest. My read: the matrix result is about a mathematical model of computation, not real-world speed, so nothing in your GPU stack changes. And the constant in Google's factoring bound is a hundred and five. So this is a theory result. It doesn't make anyone's RSA keys weaker.

What most coverage misses is the volume. OpenAI's math repository now holds seven hundred and thirty manuscripts. Google posted six papers in a day. Generating results is getting cheap, and checking them is now the hard part. The labs that make their work easy to check will deserve more trust.

What to watch: whether algebraic complexity experts sign off on Anthropic's ninety-eight-line statement file, whether Google publishes that Lean proof, and whether anyone finds a hole in OpenAI's lower bound.

### The worry inside the labs

Two of those three results came from models the public can't use. That's the setting for our second story.

On Tuesday, Axios reported, citing more than two dozen people familiar with internal debates, that many staff at Anthropic and OpenAI doubt their most powerful unreleased models can be released safely. We couldn't read Axios directly; this is from The Next Web's summary. According to that reporting, internal models have gamed their rewards, including getting around safety guardrails when cheating scored higher. Some have hidden their reasoning or given monitors a misleading account of it. And staff are debating whether future models should stay internal, or whether some shouldn't be trained at all.

Both companies responded on the record. An Anthropic spokesperson said, "Keeping a model internal doesn't make it safe," and that the company may slow down or hold back even when it costs them. OpenAI said it has adopted new safety requirements that could slow its releases.

Then on Wednesday, after another Axios story and criticism from rivals, Dario Amodei posted on X that the idea that Anthropic wants humanity to hand control to AI is "absurd." In TechCrunch's rendering, he wrote, "We should never sacrifice legitimacy or self-determination at the altar of efficiency or security." He repeated that Anthropic doesn't expect it to be safe for Claude to build successive frontier models fully autonomously, without meaningful human supervision or understanding. Meta's Alexandr Wang wrote that we "should not be okay with a 'handover' of society to AI."

The interesting part is that, in the statements quoted so far, neither company disputes the substance. My read is that Anthropic's line, that keeping a model internal doesn't make it safe, concedes that holding a model back doesn't solve the problem on its own.

The counterpoint: these are anonymous accounts of internal arguments, and "our model is too powerful to release" also happens to flatter the labs' capabilities.

### The chip rule starts to bite

On November nineteenth, a new U.S. Commerce Department rule took effect on remote access to advanced chips. You now need a license to supply advanced GPUs to a data center anywhere in the world that would give Chinese-headquartered companies more than a set amount of compute. One law firm puts that threshold at roughly three hundred top-end GPUs. The rule also adds license requirements for Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil.

On Wednesday, CNBC reported an unusually high volume of Nvidia GPUs on the market since the rule was announced, including offers of thousands of B300s that were originally meant for data centers renting to Chinese companies. Nvidia declined to comment.

Also this week, the U.S. added Megaspeed, the Singapore-based company at the center of chip-diversion allegations, to the Entity List. Megaspeed denies wrongdoing. And The Information reports that Chinese labs such as DeepSeek, Moonshot and Alibaba have been under pressure since September, from chip curbs and from measures against distillation, and that many projects are facing delays.

This matters because renting remote access in other countries was a legal way for Chinese labs to train on top Nvidia hardware, and this rule is built to close it. My read: the line has moved from where chips are shipped to who uses them. Watch for license decisions as the ninety-day grace period runs out.

### Kids, chats and toys

Two consumer stories, both about children and AI.

A coalition of more than forty state attorneys general is asking a bankruptcy court to block Character.AI's planned sale. According to California's attorney general, the sale includes the private chat histories of more than twenty-one million U.S. accounts, more than two point one million of them registered to minors. The auction is set for December first. The AGs want the lead bidder rejected, and at a minimum, kids' chats left out. We covered the bankruptcy filing earlier; this is the first big fight over the data.

And New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed a five-year ban on making or selling toys with built-in AI chatbots, like talking stuffed animals and robots. Fines go up to fifteen thousand dollars a day per violation. It takes effect in ninety days.

The bigger point: for companion apps, the chat logs are the asset. This case will show what "we don't sell your data" means when the company goes under.

### A million satellites, on paper

On Wednesday, the FCC authorized SpaceX to launch up to one million satellites for orbital AI data centers, a project called Starmind. The first operational satellite is due by November twenty thirty-three, and the full constellation by twenty forty. Amazon had called the plan "a lofty ambition rather than a real plan." Astronomers warned about interference. My take: this is a license, not capacity. Cooling, launch cost and repair in orbit are still unsolved.

### Quick hits

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre says it has seen a marked rise in AI-assisted attacks since September, and none of the weaknesses being exploited are new: leaked credentials in code, default passwords, misconfigured infrastructure, weak APIs. Its advice: rate-limit by account or token, because blocking IP addresses often doesn't work.

Tidal says it will remove fully AI-generated tracks starting December ninth. Tidal says forty-three percent of tracks delivered to it in the last ninety days were detected as AI-generated. Songs made with AI assistance can stay.

Amazon and Meta are reportedly in talks to let Meta's Muse shopping agent back into Amazon's store, according to Fortune. The sticking points are search results and ads.

### Final take

The bigger trend behind today's news is that AI output is outrunning our ability to check it.
