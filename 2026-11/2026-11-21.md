### 头条

先说头条。数学里最有名的难题之一，黎曼猜想，出现了一份由计算机逐步检查过的证明，而且是一位数学家和 Claude 一起做出来的。

先简单交代一下背景。1859 年，数学家黎曼提出了一个猜想，讲的是一个叫 zeta 函数的零点，都落在什么位置。听上去很抽象，但它决定了质数分布得有多规律。一百六十多年来，一直没有被公认的证明。

周五，在 Anthropic 工作的数学家 Levent Alpöge，发了一份简短的说明。据《科学美国人》报道，他写道，他相信自己和 Claude 一起，找到了黎曼猜想的证明。这份证明不是论文，而是用 Lean 写成的。Lean 是一种编程语言，证明里的每一步，都由计算机来检查。这份证明非常庞大，按 Lean 社区的说法，是一个 1700 万行代码的库。

周六，Lean 社区和 Mathlib 社区，发了一份联合声明，维护 Lean 的 Lean FRO，和维护 Lean 数学库的 Mathlib Initiative，都为声明背书。声明说，他们完全有信心，这份证明是正确的。理由有几条。证明的对象，是 Mathlib 里对黎曼猜想的定义，这个定义经过了专家的仔细审查。几十个人自己重新编译，结果一致。还有专门的工具确认，证明的命题和这个定义对得上。除了 Lean 自己，几个独立的检查程序，也都通过了。

另外，麻省理工学院的数学家 Andrew Sutherland，也自己做了一遍检查，并公布了结果。《科学美国人》引用他的话说，这里面出问题的可能性，几乎为零。

那 Claude 到底做了多少？据 The Decoder 对这份说明的转述，Alpöge 定了方向，在一个简化的模型上，把两种思路结合起来，之后就交给了 AI。他说，自己还没有完全掌握相关的数学，打算在接下来几个月，写一个人能读懂的版本。用的是哪一个 Claude 模型，我们没有看到说明。

还有一个插曲。Alpöge 发布几个小时后，斯坦福大学数学家、美国数学学会主席 Ravi Vakil，在自己的博客上，发布了另一份证明的草稿，作者是 Alessandro Masullo，针对的是范围更大的广义黎曼猜想。Vakil 写道，这份证明完全由人完成，没有用 AI。他也说，自己不对证明思路的正确性下结论。看了这份草稿好几个月的斯坦福数学家 Brian Conrad，对《科学美国人》说，草稿正在大幅修改。

这里有一个很多报道没讲清楚的地方。Lean 方面认证的，是经典的黎曼猜想。据报道，Alpöge 的说法更进一步，覆盖广义黎曼猜想。Sutherland 检查了其中一类情况，他的页面写明，其余部分没有检查。所以目前能放心说的是，经典的黎曼猜想，有了一份 Lean 社区信得过的机器证明。

这件事真正重要的地方在于，过去一百多年，一个证明要被接受，靠的是专家读懂、认可。这一次，是先由软件检查完逻辑，出面认证的，是维护这个检查工具的人。人还要确认一件事，就是形式化的命题，确实就是黎曼当年说的意思。Lean 的维护方说，是的。

我的判断是，在这类数学问题上，难点可能正从找到证明，转向读懂证明。一份 1700 万行的证明，已经验证过，但还没有人能解释它为什么成立。接下来要看，Lean 社区以外的数学家，认不认这个形式化命题，人能读懂的版本什么时候出来，还有广义的那部分，能不能站得住。

### Main story: the Riemann hypothesis, checked by a machine

Some background first. In eighteen fifty-nine, Bernhard Riemann made a guess about where the zeros of a function called the zeta function sit. It sounds abstract, but it controls how regularly the prime numbers are spread out. For more than a hundred and sixty years, there has been no accepted proof.

Here's what happened. On Friday, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician who works at Anthropic, posted a short note. According to Scientific American, it says: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis." The proof isn't a paper. It's written in Lean, a programming language where a computer checks every step of a proof. And it is enormous. The Lean community describes it as a library of seventeen million lines of code.

On Saturday, the Lean and Mathlib communities put out a joint statement, endorsed by the Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative, the groups behind Lean and its main math library. Quote: "We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct." They list their reasons. The statement being proved is Mathlib's own definition of the Riemann hypothesis, which they say experts vetted carefully. Dozens of people reproduced the build themselves. Two separate tools confirmed the statement matches. And several independent checkers, not just Lean's own, accepted the proof.

Separately, MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland ran his own checks and published the results. Scientific American quotes him: "Honestly the chance that anything is fishy here is approximately zero."

How much did Claude do? According to The Decoder's summary of Alpöge's note, he set the direction and combined two approaches on a simplified model, and then the AI took over. He says he hasn't fully mastered all the mathematics around it, and plans a human-readable version over the coming months. We haven't seen which Claude model was used.

Now the twist. A few hours after Alpöge's post, Stanford mathematician Ravi Vakil, the president of the American Mathematical Society, posted on his blog a separate proposed proof of a broader version, the generalized Riemann hypothesis, by Alessandro Masullo. Vakil wrote: "It is all human, with no AI." He also wrote that he makes no claim about whether the strategy is correct. Brian Conrad at Stanford, who has been reviewing it for months, told Scientific American it's "undergoing major revision."

What most headlines are missing is what exactly was certified. The Lean statement covers the classical Riemann hypothesis. Alpöge's claim reportedly goes further, to the generalized version. Sutherland's checks cover one family of those cases, and his page says the rest weren't checked. So the safe sentence is this: the classical hypothesis now has a machine-checked proof that the Lean community trusts.

Why does this matter? For a hundred and sixty years, a proof was accepted when experts read it and agreed. Here, the logic was checked by software first, and the people who certified it are the ones who maintain the checker. Humans still have to confirm one thing: that the formal statement really says what Riemann meant. The Lean maintainers say it does.

My read: the bottleneck in this kind of mathematics may be moving from finding proofs to understanding them. A seventeen-million-line proof is a verified fact without an explanation yet. Mathematicians will want to know why it's true, and what new ideas, if any, are inside it.

What to watch next: whether mathematicians outside the Lean community accept the formal statement, whether a readable version appears, and how far the generalized claim holds up.

### Two million canaries in AI prompts

This one matters for anyone building on AI APIs. HD Moore, chief research officer at the security firm Dragos, published a study on Thursday. From July through November, his team sent about two million prompts to AI providers, each containing a unique fake secret: a link, a password, or an Amazon Web Services key tied to an empty account. Then they watched for anyone using them.

Here's what they found. At least three AI hosting providers had been breached: secrets sent to them were later used to try to break into Dragos's test systems. In the biggest case, a couple of hundred fake AWS keys, each sent once to one provider in late October, were stolen. In total, the bait keys were used in tens of thousands of API calls. Moore also writes that multiple resellers on model marketplaces could view and keep customer prompts just by turning on standard logging. And some of the stolen requests had been marked zero data retention, the setting that's supposed to stop providers from keeping them.

Dragos doesn't name the providers. And keep in mind this is vendor research, from a company selling security tools.

The catch for developers is simple. When you route a prompt to whoever's cheapest, every hop in that chain can read it. Zero data retention is a promise, not a lock. Never put a live key or password in a prompt, and pick your upstream provider yourself.
