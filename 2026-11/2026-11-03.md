### 头条：Anthropic 更新招股书，一个季度收入约 170 亿美元，却亏了

先说 Anthropic。

上周我们讲过 Anthropic 递交上市申请。美国时间周一，它向美国证监会提交了修订版招股书，这次加上了第三季度的数据。

第三季度，Anthropic 收入大约 170 亿美元，比第二季度的 116 亿美元，增长了将近一半。据《财富》杂志报道，去年同期只有 14 亿美元。

但利润掉头了。第二季度，它实现经营盈利，赚了 9.74 亿美元，这也是招股书表格里，唯一一个经营盈利的季度。到了第三季度，变成经营亏损 5.67 亿美元。

原因，招股书写得很直接。亏损主要来自 50 亿美元的短期算力采购，这部分算力，比它签的多年期合同贵得多。整个季度，算力和基础设施支出接近 99 亿美元，比上一季度多了 43 亿美元。按会计口径算的净亏损，大约 233 亿美元，据《财富》报道，主要是可转换票据估值上升带来的非现金费用。

再看承诺支出。招股书列出的采购承诺和租赁，加起来大约 8954 亿美元，几乎全是算力和基础设施，其中大约 4475 亿美元，预计在 2030 年底前花出去。

还有一段话，引起了很多讨论。在风险因素部分，Anthropic 写道，它可能很快就会达到自己安全政策里，“AI 带来显著加速进展”的标准。简单说，就是 AI 开始明显加快 AI 研究本身。

同时它也写了，目前没有任何一个现有模型，被认定超过这条线。它预计，没法安全地让 Claude 完全自主地设计和打造后续模型。它承认，过去一些明显的加速，往往是发生之后才发现的。它还表示，正在考虑更新这套安全政策。

另外，招股书里还有一个数字。按标价计算，Anthropic 内部自己用 Claude 的价值，从 2026 年 1 月到 9 月，涨了 250 多倍，今年 4 月，这个月度数字，第一次超过了它每月不含股权激励的人员开支。

我的判断是，这次亏损，更像是采购问题，不是需求问题。需求涨得比提前锁定的便宜算力还快，只好去买贵的短期算力。签长约能便宜，但代价就是，背上将近 9000 亿美元的长期承诺。

至于 AI 加速 AI 的那段话，要认真看，但不要过度解读。风险因素是法律披露，一般写得很宽，“可能很快”说的是对未来模型的内部估计，不是现有模型已经做到了什么。真正的新意在于，一家 AI 公司把自己设定的这条线，正式写进了给监管机构的文件里。

接下来要看的，是还空着的发行价区间，还有它说要更新的安全政策，会不会在上市前公布。

### 次要新闻：让 AI 直接改另一个 AI 的权重

第二条，一项很有意思的研究。

周一，麻省理工计算机与人工智能实验室的几位研究者发了一篇博客，让 OpenAI 的 GPT-6 Astra，直接读写神经网络的权重。不训练，不跑梯度下降，模型看着参数，直接动手改。

最受关注的实验，用的是一个 8 亿参数的小模型，Qwen 3.5。这个模型经常陷入重复循环。Astra 在 Python 环境里，把问题追到两层网络里的一个方向，然后把它减掉。总共只改了百分之零点零一六的参数，数学测试 MATH-500 的成绩，从百分之七点九，提到了百分之四十八点一。

它还能只看原始权重，判断一个小游戏策略网络是在哪个游戏上训练的。还直接从零写出一个打砖块游戏的网络，得分 517 分左右，用常规方法训练 1 亿步的网络，只有 16 分左右。

作者也说得很清楚，这还很早期。模型都很小，换成另一个小模型 Gemma，它就没能改好。Qwen 的提升，主要是去掉了循环这个毛病，不是教会了它新的数学能力。所有实验记录都公开了。

我更关注的是监管这一面。不经过训练，直接改权重，这种改动更难审计。和上一条放在一起看，问题是同一个，模型开始改模型的时候，人怎么把关。

### 次要新闻：纽约时报起诉 Brave，矛头指向 AI 的数据来源

第三条，版权官司。

周一，《纽约时报》在纽约曼哈顿联邦法院起诉了 Brave，就是做浏览器和搜索引擎的那家公司。《纽约每日新闻》，《芝加哥论坛报》和《奥兰多哨兵报》，另外单独起诉。

以下都是原告的指控。他们说，Brave 抓取新闻内容，建自己的搜索索引，而且一直不公开爬虫的名字，媒体想屏蔽都没办法。然后，Brave 通过搜索 API，把索引卖给 AI 公司。诉状点名的客户，包括 Anthropic，Perplexity，Mistral，xAI 和 Cohere。诉状还指控，Brave 的 AI 问答功能，会逐字复述文章，它的付费墙拦截功能，让用户不用订阅就能看。原告要求赔偿，并要求法院禁止这些行为。目前没有看到 Brave 的回应。

这件事真正重要的地方在于，过去媒体主要告的是做大模型的公司，这次告的是中间的供应商，也就是卖搜索数据给 AI 的那一层。如果你在做联网搜索的 Agent，这其实就是你的供应链。

### 次要新闻：AI 基建，缺钱也缺电

第四条，两件事放在一起看。

先说钱。周一，甲骨文向美国证监会提交文件，计划发行 100 亿美元股票，和 100 亿美元优先票据，用于资本开支，偿还债务和投资。这在它上周的投资者日上已经预告过，但据 CNBC 报道，当天股价还是跌了将近百分之九。甲骨文的债务大约 1253 亿美元，今年 7 月，标普把它的评级下调到 BBB-，只比垃圾级高一档。

再说电。OpenAI 推出了一个叫 Grid Protectors，也就是“电网守护者”的计划，以后它自己建的数据中心园区，在电网吃紧时，会主动减少用电。第一个试点，是佐治亚州一个还在开发中的园区，和当地电力公司的协议里，最多可以削减 1000 兆瓦用电，OpenAI 说，这相当于大约 70 万户家庭的平均用电。它还承诺，自己承担园区所需的全部电网和供电费用。同时 OpenAI 也承认，这种灵活调节，替代不了新建电厂和输电线路。

我的判断是，钱和电，就是这轮 AI 基建的两道坎。借钱越来越贵，地方上对电价的反对也越来越多。主动削减用电，是建数据中心的公司，最容易给出的让步，但只有条款公开，真正执行，才有意义。

### 快讯

接下来是快讯。

高盛。据彭博社报道，高盛把 Claude Code 和 GitHub 的 AI 编程工具，基本限制在工程师范围内，大部分交易员和银行家不能再用了。发言人说，原因包括效率和风险管理，试验阶段已经结束。今年 7 月，高盛的首席信息官还公开夸过非技术员工用 AI 写代码。

OpenAI 发布了一个新评测，叫 LifeVal，测 AI 能不能帮人处理日常事务，比如找托儿服务，做预算，办政府手续。题目参考了时间使用调查，还有一份将近 4000 人的问卷。OpenAI 说，它最新的模型在很多任务上得分很高。这是厂商自己的评测，页面也没说题目是否公开。

最后，今天是美国中期选举投票日。据 Semafor 报道，OpenAI 和 Anthropic 都第一次设立了选举应急小组，监测平台滥用和错误的投票信息。昨天我们讲过，有测试发现，聊天机器人回答选举问题，将近百分之四十有错。投票信息，还是要以官方网站为准。

### Hook

Anthropic made almost seventeen billion dollars in revenue last quarter, and still lost money running the business. The reason is compute it had to buy at short notice. The same filing also includes a warning about AI speeding up AI research, which is not the kind of thing you usually find in an IPO document.

### Today's top stories

Also today: MIT researchers had a frontier model rewrite another model's weights directly, the New York Times sues Brave over the search data AI companies buy, and two reminders that the AI build-out runs on money and electricity.

This is Daily AI Signal for Tuesday, November third. Let's get into it.

### Main story — Anthropic's amended IPO filing

Last week we covered Anthropic's first public IPO filing. On Monday, it filed an amended version with the SEC, and this one includes the third quarter.

Here's what changed. Revenue for the three months to the end of September was about seventeen billion dollars. That's up nearly half from eleven point six billion in the second quarter, and Fortune notes it was one point four billion a year earlier. Back in October, CNBC and others had reported a third-quarter figure of sixteen point eight billion, based on numbers shared with investors. The filing comes in slightly higher.

But the second quarter was the only quarter in the filing's tables with an operating profit, nine hundred seventy-four million dollars. In the third quarter it swung to an operating loss of five hundred sixty-seven million.

The filing gives the main reason. The loss was primarily driven by five billion dollars of compute bought under short-term commitments, which, in the filing's words, currently costs significantly more than compute bought under its multi-year deals. Compute and infrastructure expense came to about nine point nine billion dollars for the quarter, up more than four billion from the quarter before. There was also about two billion dollars of non-cash charges tied to an employee donation-matching program. And the net loss was about twenty-three point three billion, which Fortune says is mostly an accounting charge on the rising value of its convertible notes.

Then there's the size of the commitments. Anthropic now lists purchase commitments and leases worth roughly eight hundred ninety-five billion dollars in total, nearly all of it compute and infrastructure. About four hundred forty-seven billion of that is expected before the end of twenty thirty. Reports on the filing say that includes up to about one hundred twenty billion dollars of compute from SpaceXAI, the former xAI.

The part that got the most attention is in the risk factors. Anthropic writes that it may imminently meet its own Responsible Scaling Policy definition of, quote, "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI." In plain terms, AI speeding up AI research. It also says it has not designated any of its current models as exceeding that threshold. It says it does not expect to be able to safely let Claude design and build future models fully on its own. It admits that in the past it hasn't spotted big accelerations until after they happened. And it says it's considering updates to its Responsible Scaling Policy.

One more line from the filing: the value of Anthropic's own internal Claude use, priced at list rates, grew more than two hundred fifty times between January and September. In April, that monthly value first passed what Anthropic spends each month on its people, not counting stock-based pay.

What most headlines are missing. The loss looks like a procurement problem more than a demand problem. Demand outran the long-term compute Anthropic had lined up, so it bought expensive short-term capacity to keep up. Long contracts are cheaper, but they're how you end up with nearly nine hundred billion dollars of obligations. That's the trade every frontier lab is making, and this filing shows what it can cost in a single quarter.

On the self-improvement language, my read is to take it seriously but not literally. A risk factor is a legal disclosure. Companies write them broadly, and "may imminently" here is about internal estimates for future models, not something a current model has done. What's new is that a lab has put its own threshold on the record with securities regulators, where misstatements carry legal risk.

The limits: these are company-reported interim numbers, part of the loss is one-off and non-cash, and roughly two hundred fourteen billion of those commitments, per reporting on the filing, carry termination rights or conditions.

What to watch next: the price range, which is still blank, and whether Anthropic publishes the Responsible Scaling Policy changes it says it's considering before the shares start trading.

### Secondary — An AI that edits another AI's weights

This one is from MIT CSAIL. In a blog post on Monday, four researchers, including Omar Khattab, showed OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra reading and writing neural network weights directly. No training loop and no gradient descent. The model just looks at the numbers and changes them.

Some of the results. Given only raw weights, it could tell which game a small game-playing policy had been trained on, and which language a small transformer had learned. It wrote weights from scratch for an Atari-style Breakout network that scored about five hundred seventeen, against about sixteen for a network trained the usual way for a hundred million steps.

The headline experiment uses Qwen three point five, a small open model with zero point eight billion parameters. Working in a Python environment, Astra traced the model's habit of getting stuck in loops to one direction inside two of its layers, and subtracted it. That changed sixteen thousandths of one percent of the weights. Its score on the MATH-500 benchmark went from seven point nine percent to forty-eight point one.

The catch, and the authors are upfront about it: this is very early. The models are small. It couldn't spot a deliberately sycophantic Gemma model from its weights alone, and it couldn't improve Gemma. And the Qwen gain mostly comes from removing a failure, the looping, not from teaching new math. In the authors' words, editing weights could remove an obstacle that hid an ability, but it could not add an ability the model lacked. They've released full logs of every experiment.

My read: this matters beyond interpretability. Changes made directly to the weights, outside of training, are harder to audit. Put that next to Anthropic's filing and you get the same question from two directions: what oversight looks like when models start working on models.

### Secondary — Publishers sue Brave over AI's search supply chain

On Monday, the New York Times sued Brave Software in federal court in Manhattan. The Daily News, the Chicago Tribune and the Orlando Sentinel filed a separate suit.

These are allegations. The complaints say Brave scrapes journalism for its search index, never disclosed its crawler's name, so publishers couldn't block it, and sells access to that index through its Search API. According to the reporting, the complaints name Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI and Cohere as API customers. They also target Brave's AI answer feature, which they say reproduces passages word for word, and its paywall blocker. The papers want damages and an injunction. A Times spokesperson said Brave "has flouted established copyright law." We haven't seen a response from Brave. Brave has argued in an earlier case against News Corp that indexing is fair use.

Why it matters: publishers have mostly sued the model makers. This goes after the supplier in the middle, the search index sold as an API that a lot of AI answers are built on. If you build web-grounded agents, this is your supply chain. If the theory holds, web access gets more expensive for everyone.

### Secondary — Money and power

Two stories from Monday about what the AI build-out runs on.

Money first. Oracle filed to sell ten billion dollars of stock and ten billion of senior notes, to fund capital spending, debt repayment and investments. It had previewed this at its investor day last week, where it capped its extra market funding through fiscal twenty thirty at twenty billion. The market didn't like it anyway: CNBC says the stock fell almost nine percent. Oracle carries about one hundred twenty-five billion dollars of debt, and S&P cut it to BBB-minus in July, one notch above junk.

Then power. OpenAI launched what it calls the Grid Protectors Program. Campuses it builds itself will cut their electricity use when the local grid is under strain. The first is a planned campus in Effingham County, Georgia, where the deal with Georgia Power includes up to one thousand megawatts of flexible demand. OpenAI compares that to the average use of about seven hundred thousand homes. It says it will pay the full cost of the lines and service the campus needs. It also says flexibility can't replace building new power plants and transmission.

My read: these are the two limits on the build-out. Lenders now want to be paid more for AI risk, and local communities are pushing back on power bills. Demand response is the cheapest concession a builder can offer. It only means something if the curtailment terms are public and enforced, and so far only Georgia's are.

### Quick hits

Goldman Sachs has restricted Claude Code and GitHub's coding tools mainly to its engineers, Bloomberg reports. Most traders and bankers lost access. A spokesperson cited productivity and risk management and said the experimentation phase is over. That's a reversal from July, when the bank's CIO praised vibe coding by non-technical staff.

OpenAI published LifeVal, an evaluation of everyday tasks like childcare, budgeting and government paperwork. It built it from national time-use studies and a survey of almost four thousand people. OpenAI says its latest models score very highly on many of the tasks. That's a vendor eval, and the page doesn't say whether the task set is public.

And it's election day in the US. Semafor reports OpenAI and Anthropic are both running election war rooms for the first time, monitoring for misuse and wrong voting information. Yesterday we covered a test that found errors in almost forty percent of chatbot answers on election questions. So again: check your official election site.

### Final take

The bigger trend behind today's news is that the cost of AI is showing up on paper. A seventeen-billion-dollar quarter that still lost money on compute. Eight hundred ninety-five billion in commitments. Oracle paying more to borrow. A power deal written to calm local opposition. The one thing I'd watch next is Anthropic's price range, because that's when public markets put a number on all of it.
